## Slide 30: Were we transforming the right thing?

- Accessibility: Every person in the chain handles a Word document with barriers.

- Governance: Oversight committees work from incomplete data. Staff shortages mean only two of all sections get transferred into the spreadsheet.

**Image:** FSS Postgraduate Research Student Application Decision Form

**Image:** Visiting Research Student Application Form

**Image:** Compliance and Selection Requirements Workbook. This one features a table within a table.

### Speaker notes

Even after the Word form is more accessible than it was, is fixing the Word form the right thing to fix?

Look at what one of these external examiner forms goes through. Between 11 and 15 email handoffs to complete a single report. Copies land in seven or more storage locations, every team in the chain keeping their own. There is no single source of truth.

Then the governance dimension. QSAT manually copy and paste content into Excel spreadsheets from the Word files, which eventually reaches oversight committees. Staff shortages mean only two sections of each form get copied into the spreadsheet they review. So senior committees might make decisions on partial data, because nobody had the time to extract more.

So, for every 100 programmes with 2 external examiners that would be 2,600 emails, 1,800 document copies in inboxes, potentially 400 copies and pastes.

What would be better is an accessible web form, with a database behind it. Data entered once. It flows through the approval workflow with no email handoffs or chasing. The oversight spreadsheet populates itself from the source data, with no copying and pasting.

The accessibility fix, the efficiency fix, and the governance fix could have been the same project.

There are many more examples like this; our university seems to run on inaccessible Word-based forms being emailed between students, staff and externals.

## Slide 31: Same scaffolding, two users

Screen readers and LLM browser agents both navigate via the accessibility tree: the structured representation the browser builds from semantic HTML and ARIA.

- Screen reader users: Need machine-readable semantics to know what each element is and does. Without proper markup, navigation becomes guesswork.
- LLM agents: Need the same machine-readable semantics to act reliably. Production examples: ChatGPT Atlas, Playwright MCP.

An accessible redesign serves both audiences at once.

### Speaker notes

There's an extra point that strengthens the case. Screen readers and AI browser agents work the same way. They both navigate a web page by reading something called the accessibility tree. That's the structured representation the browser builds from the semantic HTML and ARIA attributes on the page. The visual styling doesn't reach them. They work from the structure.

A screen reader user needs that structure to know what each element is and what it does. Without it, they're guessing.

A modern AI agent like ChatGPT Atlas or Playwright MCP needs the same structure to do its job. If your page is built so a screen reader can use it, an AI agent can use it too.

If the university were to engineer the External Examiner form as an accessible web form, we also create the conditions for an AI agent to act on behalf of the user. Two audiences served by one redesign. That's before we even think about automating the data flows, reminders, data pattern analysis and other aspects that could massively improve the operational efficiency and institutional effectiveness of this process.

## Slide 38: Barriers sit in systems. Inclusive data flows keep them from forming.

**Preferred names**

Specify for each data integration whether the system needs the legal name or the preferred first name.

Example: dead-naming for trans students, or name changes for safety.

**Data minimisation**

Send each system only the data it needs for its business function, with no default inheritance of every field.

Example: does the system need to know a person's title?

### Speaker notes

Joshie presents the first part.

That carries straight into the second box on the slide. The principle of data minimisation: each downstream system should only receive the data it needs to do its job.

For example, does the timetabling system need to know whether you're a Mr or a Ms or a Dr? No. Does the alumni database need to? Different question. The discipline is to make that decision system by system rather than push every field everywhere by default.

This is the work that sits under Goal 1 of our Digital Strategic Plan, the Identity and Access Management project. Done well, it builds inclusion into the foundations. Done by default, it cascades whatever harm exists in the source data through every system that subscribes.

## Slide 43: The wider barrier

Handwritten content blocks supports that many disabled groups need.

**The barrier: An image carries no structure**

Handwritten, scanned, or photographed content is invisible to assistive technology. It cannot be read aloud, resized, recoloured, or passed on for further processing.

**What gets unlocked: Many supports require structured digital content**

Text-to-speech, screen magnification, font and colour changes, EPUB readers for Kindle, translation, search, and integration with other assistive technology: all need structure to work, and many disabled groups depend on them.

**Maths, the hardest case: The structured form for maths is MathML**

Reading an equation out as a flat sentence is impossible to parse. MathML provides hierarchical semantic notation that lets a blind student navigate element by element.

### Speaker notes

This goes wider than blind students. Take any handwritten, scanned, or photographed content. It is an image. To assistive technology, an image is opaque.

Translation tools, EPUB readers, text-to-speech engines all need structured text to operate. Take the structure away and you take access away from most of the assistive technology stack.

Maths is the hardest case. A blind student needs to move through the equation, element by element, the way a sighted student visually scans the formula. The structured form that allows that is MathML. With it in place, not only will blind students benefit, it becomes accessible to all assistive technologies.
